# EXHAUSTIVE EXTRACTION: THE SETTLERS OF CATAN (CATAN)

### 1. SNAPSHOT

**Designer:** Klaus Teuber
**Publisher:** Kosmos (German)/Catan Studio (International)
**First Released:** 1995 (Germany as "Die Siedler von Catan")
**Current Player Count:** 3-4 players (base game)
**Recommended Age:** 10+ (also Catan Junior 6+)
**Average Play Time:** 45-120 minutes (45-60 minutes experienced players, 90-120 minutes new players)
**Complexity Weight (BGG):** 2.29/5 (light-to-moderate)
**Sales & Awards:**
- **Spiel des Jahres (Game of the Year):** 1995
- **All-time Sales:** Over 40 million boxed sets sold worldwide (as of 2024)
- **Translations:** 40+ languages
- **Impact:** Landmark "gateway game" that introduced modern Eurogames to mainstream audiences; launched the Eurogame revolution and catalyzed a shift toward resource management, negotiation-based, and strategic board gaming

**Why It's Landmark:** Catan transcended the hobby, becoming the defining gateway game that exposed millions to modern board gaming. It demonstrates that games could blend strategy with negotiation, combine elegant simplicity with strategic depth, and remain fresh across countless plays through randomization. Klaus Teuber's design philosophy—emphasizing beautiful components, iterative development, and a balance between luck and skill—established a template for Eurogame success that persists today.

---

### 2. CORE FANTASY

Players assume the role of settlers establishing and expanding colonies on the undiscovered island of Catan. The fantasy is one of **pioneering, resource gathering, and community-building**: you arrive with nothing, harvest raw materials from the land (wood from forests, brick from hills, ore from mountains, wheat from fields, sheep from pastures), negotiate and trade with rival settlers, and progressively build an infrastructure of roads, settlements, and cities. The game embodies the Age of Discovery narrative of exploration and settlement, grounded in Klaus Teuber's childhood fascination with maps and Viking/settler history.

---

### 3. GOAL & WIN/LOSE CONDITIONS

**Primary Goal:** Accumulate **10 Victory Points (VPs)** before any opponent.

**Victory Point Sources:**
- Each **settlement** = 1 VP (max 5 per player)
- Each **city** (upgrade) = 2 VPs (max 4 per player; replaces a settlement, netting +1 VP)
- **Longest Road** card = 2 VPs (requires 5+ continuous roads; can be stolen if an opponent builds 6+ roads)
- **Largest Army** card = 2 VPs (requires playing 3+ Knight development cards; can be stolen if an opponent plays 4+ knights)
- **Development cards** (University, Chapel, Library, Market, Scholar) = 1 VP each (some cards)

**Game End:**
- Game ends immediately when any player reaches **10 VPs** and is announced as the winner.
- Victory is communal in that all players can see VP progress, creating visible tension and allowing other players to adjust strategies (e.g., blocking the leader's expansion via robber placement).

**Lose Condition:** Explicit loss is not defined; players who reach 0 VPs do not "lose"—the game simply continues until a player reaches 10. However, in practice, a player trailing significantly is effectively eliminated from contention.

### 4. FULL RULES & TURN STRUCTURE

#### Board Setup

**Physical Layout:**
- The game board is constructed from 19 hexagonal terrain tiles arranged in a specific pattern (creating an island).
- Each hex represents one of five terrain types:
  - **Forest** (produces lumber/wood) — 4 hexes
  - **Hills** (produces brick) — 3 hexes
  - **Mountains** (produces ore) — 3 hexes
  - **Fields** (produces wheat/grain) — 4 hexes
  - **Pasture** (produces sheep/wool) — 4 hexes
  - **Desert** (produces nothing) — 1 hex (the robber starts here)

**Randomization:**
- Terrain hexes are shuffled and placed randomly within the board frame.
- Number tokens (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12; note: 7 is omitted) are randomly placed on non-desert hexes.
- Constraint: 6 and 8 should not be placed adjacent to avoid unfair advantage (though this is optional).
- Harbor tokens (generic 3:1 and resource-specific 2:1) are randomly distributed along coastal edges.

**Initial Settlement Placement:**
- Players place settlements and roads in turn order (then reverse order for the second round of placement).
- Each player places 1 settlement and 1 road in the first round, then immediately places a second settlement and road in the second round (in reverse order).
- This ensures early-game balance while giving later placement positions slight mid-game advantage.

#### Turn Structure (Three Phases in Sequence)

**Phase 1: Roll for Resource Production**

1. The active player rolls two standard six-sided dice.
2. The sum (2-12) determines which terrain hexes produce resources.
3. **Resource Distribution:**
   - Each player owning a settlement on an edge of a hex with the rolled number receives **1 resource card** of that hex's type.
   - Each player owning a city on that hex receives **2 resource cards**.
   - If a player has no settlements/cities on producing hexes, they receive nothing.

4. **Special Case—Rolling a 7:**
   - No resources are produced.
   - All players holding more than 7 resource cards must discard half their hand (rounded down).
   - The active player moves the robber to any non-desert hex of their choice.
   - The robber blocks that hex from producing resources until moved again.
   - The active player steals 1 random resource card from any player with a settlement or city touching the robber's new location.

**Phase 2: Trade**

During this phase, the active player (and only the active player) may engage in trades. Other players may not trade with each other.

- **Domestic Trade:** Trade resource cards with other players on any mutually agreed terms.
- **Maritime Trade (Bank/Port Trade):**
  - **4:1 Ratio (Universal):** Trade 4 identical resources to the bank for 1 resource of choice.
  - **3:1 Ratio (Generic Harbor):** If the player has a settlement or city on a 3:1 harbor, trade 3 identical resources for 1 resource of choice.
  - **2:1 Ratio (Specialized Harbor):** If the player has a settlement or city on a resource-specific harbor (e.g., grain harbor), trade 2 of that resource for 1 resource of choice. This ratio applies only to that specific resource.

**Phase 3: Build**

The active player may spend resource cards to build, buy development cards, or upgrade settlements to cities. Actions are optional and not taken in any mandatory order.

- **Building a Road:** Costs 1 brick + 1 lumber. Roads must connect end-to-end from existing roads or settlements. A player can build a maximum of 15 roads.
- **Building a Settlement:** Costs 1 brick + 1 lumber + 1 sheep + 1 wheat. A settlement must be placed on an intersection that is connected to one of the player's roads, at least 2 intersections away from any other settlement, and on a vacant intersection. Max 5 settlements.
- **Upgrading to a City:** Costs 3 ore + 2 wheat. A city replaces an existing settlement, increasing that intersection's value from 1 VP to 2 VPs and doubling resource production from hexes it touches. Max 4 cities.
- **Buying a Development Card:** Costs 1 sheep + 1 wheat + 1 ore. The player draws a card from the development deck.

**End of Turn:** Pass the dice to the left player. Play proceeds clockwise.

#### The Five Resources
1. **Lumber/Wood** (Forest) — roads and settlements.
2. **Brick** (Hills) — roads and settlements.
3. **Sheep/Wool** (Pasture) — settlements and development cards.
4. **Wheat/Grain** (Fields) — settlements, cities, development cards.
5. **Ore** (Mountains) — cities and development cards.

#### Development Cards (25-card deck)
- **14 Knight Cards:** move the robber + steal; 3+ played = Largest Army (2 VPs).
- **5 Victory Point Cards:** each worth 1 VP.
- **2 Road Building:** place 2 free roads.
- **2 Year of Plenty:** take 2 resources from the bank.
- **2 Monopoly:** name a resource; all opponents give you all of theirs.
- A card bought on one turn cannot be played until a later turn.

#### The Robber
Starts on desert; moves on a rolled 7; blocks a hex from producing; lets the mover steal 1 random card from an adjacent opponent; primary catch-up tool (leader usually targeted) and a diplomacy/bargaining chip.

#### Longest Road & Largest Army
- **Longest Road (2 VPs):** first to a continuous 5+ road; stolen if beaten.
- **Largest Army (2 VPs):** first to play 3 knights; stolen if beaten.

### 5. ECONOMIC / RESOURCE SYSTEMS

#### Asymmetric Scarcity
- **Abundant (4 hexes each):** Lumber, Wheat, Sheep
- **Scarce (3 hexes each):** Brick, Ore
Ore and brick are more valuable because scarcer; early settlements on mountain/hill hexes confer structural advantage.

#### Production via Dice Probability (bell curve)
- **7:** 16.67% (6/36) · **6, 8:** 13.89% each · **5, 9:** 11.11% · **4, 10:** 8.33% · **3, 11:** 5.56% · **2, 12:** 2.78%.
Settling on 6/7/8-marked hexes maximizes production; the robber blocks the most-rolled numbers for maximum disruption.

#### Trading Economy
1. **Domestic (player-to-player):** zero-cost, any agreed ratio, on the active player's turn; bartering, bluffing, refusing.
2. **Maritime:** 4:1 universal · 3:1 generic harbor · 2:1 specialized harbor; the fallback when negotiation fails.

#### Supply/Demand Dynamics
Resource value shifts over time: early game lumber/brick dominate (roads/settlements); mid game sheep/wheat rise (settlements, dev cards); late game ore peaks (cities, dev cards, final push). Scarce-resource holders gain negotiating leverage.

---

### 6. DECISION DYNAMICS

- **Meaningful choices:** settlement placement (the cascading decision), resource targeting, trading partners/rates, build priorities (expand vs upgrade vs dev cards), knight timing, robber targeting (a kingmaking moment).
- **Luck vs skill:** ~50/50 (experts reduce luck's influence). Teuber explicitly designed a balance between strategy and luck so newcomers can win on luck while skill is rewarded.
- **Robber:** primary negative interaction — tactical (block/steal), diplomatic (bargaining), catch-up (target the leader).
- **Negotiation & table talk:** the social heart — proposals, counters, refusals, alliances, bluffs.
- **Catch-up:** player-driven negative feedback (robber on leader, refuse trades, block expansion, monopoly swings) — but this enables **kingmaking**.
- **Blocking/denial:** settlement & road placement, withholding trades, dev cards.

---

### 7. PROGRESSION / GROWTH

- **Light engine building:** settlements/cities form a production engine; cities double output.
- **Consolidation vs expansion:** double down on good hexes (cities) vs spread settlements to diversify.
- **Compounding advantage:** best starting position snowballs, but limited by hex scarcity, the robber, and dev-card disruption.
- **Production pyramid:** target heavy ore + wheat, moderate sheep, baseline brick/lumber.
- **Strategy shifts by phase:** infrastructure → scaling (cities/dev cards/Longest Road/Largest Army) → final push.

---

### 8. TENSION, DRAMA & FUN

- **Trading table talk:** shouted proposals, intense negotiations, broken promises.
- **Rolling a 7:** dreaded/anticipated; volatile; triggers robber diplomacy.
- **Racing for points:** visible VPs; the leader gets scrutinized/blocked; surprise dev-card VPs.
- **Robber emotional core:** frustrating + fun; creates a narrative arc each game.
- **The compelling loop:** roll (uncertain) → trade (negotiate) → build (decisive) — fast, repeatable, keeps non-active players engaged.

---

### 9. REPLAYABILITY ENGINE

- **Randomized board each game** (hexes + number tokens + harbors) → unique strategic puzzles; defeats memorized openings.
- **Variable setups:** beginner (fixed) vs advanced (random).
- **Expansions:** Seafarers (ships/exploration), Cities & Knights (commodities/knights/barbarians/progress cards, 13 VP goal, deeper & less luck-dependent); combinable.
- **Why fresh:** randomization > memorization; different opponents; dice variance; modular board; scalable depth.

---

### 10. COMPONENTS & PHYSICAL DESIGN

Box: modular 19-hex board, 18 number tokens, harbors, robber, per-player wooden settlements/cities/roads, 95 resource cards, 25 dev cards, 2 dice, 16-page rulebook. Premium **wooden** pieces; striking hexagonal board; clear iconography (low text dependency); ~5-min setup for experienced players. Teachable in ~15 min; first game 90–120 min, dropping to 45–60.

---

### 11. ACCESSIBILITY & SCALING

- **3 players:** faster, higher variance. **4 players:** optimal, rich negotiation. **5–6:** via expansion, more downtime. **8+:** chaotic, needs multi-copies.
- **Ages:** 10+ recommended (Catan Junior 6+).
- **Low cognitive load:** transparent info (visible VPs; inferable hands).
- **Runaway leader / kingmaking:** feedback is player-driven not mechanical → coalition risk; weaker catch-up at 3 players.

---

### 12. CRITICISMS & WEAKNESSES

- **Dice luck dependency** (fair for casual; experts mitigate).
- **Kingmaking** ("beat the leader" coalitions).
- **Trading lulls / negotiation downtime** (worse at 5+).
- **Robber frustration / can feel unfair** (intentional chaos).
- **Weak at 2 players;** limited catch-up at 3.

---

### 13. TRANSFERABLE DESIGN PRINCIPLES FOR AN AI-BUILDING GAME

**Emulate**
1. **Resource scarcity creates decision tension** → make compute/power/data scarce with varying abundance.
2. **Probabilistic production + player agency** → randomized outcomes, but players position to optimize expected returns.
3. **Trading as social centerpiece** → enable player-to-player resource trades (compute↔data).
4. **Player-agency catch-up** → let trailing players ally to block a runaway leader's scarce resources.
5. **Modular randomized setup** → randomize the "market environment" (which chips/power/regulations are scarce) each game.
6. **Public progress** → visible performance leaderboard creates tension.
7. **Asymmetric power cards** → breakthroughs/regulatory approvals as limited cards.
8. **Negative feedback loop** → a "regulator/market saturation" that blocks leaders, ideally with a negotiation hook.
9. **Light engine building** → scaling laws (exponential early, diminishing late); reward early efficiency.
10. **Multiple win paths** → market dominance / research breakthrough / regulatory approval, to avoid solved play.

**Avoid**
1. **Excessive dice luck** over long sessions.
2. **Kingmaking without safeguards** (consider mechanical catch-up).
3. **Downtime scaling poorly** (use simultaneous actions for 5+).
4. **Unstructured negotiation** (scaffold with ports/auctions/trading posts).
5. **Purely punitive blocking** (make it negotiable).
6. **Hidden info overload** (favor transparency to reduce AP).
7. **Runaway learning curve** (core rules learnable in 15–20 min).
8. **Static board → memorized strategies** (ensure variability/procedural setup).
